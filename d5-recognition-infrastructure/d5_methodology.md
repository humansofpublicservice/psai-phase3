## Methodology of the 2026 rebuild

*Technical record. Written for a reader who will use PSAI results and needs to
judge whether to trust them.*

## 1. Summary

Dimension 5 measures whether a state has **recognition infrastructure** — laws,
policies, award programs, and documented recognition activity for its career
employees. It is 5% of the PSAI composite.

The previous version of D5 failed a data-quality audit and was rebuilt from
scratch rather than repaired. The audit's central finding: 40% of every state's
D5 score came from a component that measured *HOPS's own publishing output*
rather than anything the state does, and **not one of 51 jurisdictions had a
source a reader could follow.**

The rebuild replaced continuous judgment scores with **six binary,
evidence-backed indicators**. Every point traces to a URL that was fetched and
read. Every answer is one of three values — found, confirmed absent, or couldn't
check — and the third is what makes guessing structurally impossible. Scores are
generated by a script from the evidence file; no score is typed by hand anywhere.

**Headline result:** 306 evidence rows across 51 jurisdictions. 155 rows earned
points, and **92.3% of those rest on a state's own published document** (143 of
155; the other 12 are news reports that explicitly state a document exists or an
event occurred). 24 jurisdictions were fully resolved on all six indicators, 26
have at least one indicator that could not be checked, and 1 — Vermont — could
not be scored at all because every one of its source domains refused every
request.

---

## 2. What D5 measures, and its weight

Recognition Infrastructure asks a narrow question: **does the state have the
apparatus to recognize the people who work for it, and is that apparatus
currently in use?** Not whether employees feel valued, and not whether the
recognition is generous — see §9 for what is deliberately out of scope.

D5 carries **5% of the PSAI composite.** That has a specific consequence worth
stating plainly, and the old audit quantified it:

- Moving a state's D5 score by the full available range changes its composite by at most 5 points.
- The single largest error found in the old data — North Carolina, off by 23.9 D5 points — moved its composite by **1.19 points.**

A 5% dimension **will not change who is at the top or bottom of the index.** It
will change who sits 12th versus 16th. In the old master file, ranks 11 through
14 spanned just **0.4 composite points**, so a 1.2-point swing reorders the
middle of the table by several places.

That is the reason accuracy matters here. D5 is cheap to be wrong about in
absolute score and expensive to be wrong about in **rank order** — and rank
order is what gets published and quoted.

## 3. What was wrong with the old D5

All figures in this section come from `dimension5_audit_report.md`. The audit
opened one workbook per jurisdiction, all 51, plus the master index candidates.

### The formula measured the wrong thing

Every workbook used:

```
D5 = 0.40 × HOPS story density + 0.35 × formal programs + 0.25 × observances
```

**HOPS story density counts how many stories HOPS has published about a state.**
That is a measure of our own collection effort, not of the state's recognition
infrastructure — and it carried the largest weight in the dimension. A state with
no HOPS coverage was scored as having weak recognition infrastructure.

The audit called this "the single largest methodological problem in the
dimension," and noted it could not be fixed by re-checking values, because the
metric was measuring the wrong subject.

Two individual attributions made it worse. **New Mexico** scored a HOPS story
about the Chief Financial Officer of Los Alamos **County** — not a state
employee. **North Dakota** scored a story its own file describes as about "an ND
**native**; no state-employee stories found."

### Most values were not sourced, and none were checkable

| Measure | Result |
|---|---|
| Values marked DIRECT (stated in the file) | 28 of 51 — **55%** |
| Values marked INFERRED (assumed, estimated, proxy, placeholder) | 22 of 51 — **43%** |
| Values marked UNKNOWN | 1 of 51 — 2% |
| **Jurisdictions with a fetchable URL for any D5 value** | **0 of 51 — 0%** |
| Jurisdictions naming no D5 source at all | 6 (Kentucky, Minnesota, Nebraska, North Carolina, South Dakota, Washington) |
| Jurisdictions recording no source year | 31 of 51 |

Citations appeared as opaque internal tokens like `【599618054233774†L24-L27】` —
retrieval artifacts that resolve to nothing. The audit's verification counts
were therefore **0 MATCH, 0 MISMATCH, and 44 NO_FETCHABLE_URL**: nothing could
be confirmed or refuted.

### Placeholder zeros stored as measurements

**HOPS story density was 0 for 41 of 51 jurisdictions — 80%.** Those zeros were
not findings about the states. The workbooks say so: "No HOPS stories found,"
"Data Not Published," "HOPS pending." Maryland labelled its D5 **"provisional
(HOPS pending)"** and Virginia recorded **"Awaiting HOPS story counts /
Placeholder"** — and both were published as scores.

There was no separate field distinguishing "this state runs no programs" from
"we did not find out." Both were stored as `0`.

### Values that were admitted guesses

- **Ohio** — "Assumed 2 stories due to lack of database access." Ohio's D5 of 47.8 was the 4th highest in the country and rested partly on that guess.
- **Illinois** — source column reads "HOPS database (assumed)."
- **South Dakota** — both scored components estimated with no documentary evidence.
- **Wyoming** — "Assumed common gubernatorial proclamation; no direct evidence found." The primary evidence for its recognition ceremony was a Flickr album.
- **Oklahoma** — its only proclamation was from **2008**, with "similar proclamations issued annually (assumed)." Eighteen years old.

### Two different numbers for a quarter of the country

The published master CSV and the underlying workbooks disagreed for **12
jurisdictions — 24%** — with no rule for choosing between them. The largest gaps:

| Jurisdiction | Master CSV | Workbook | Gap |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| North Carolina | 53.9 | 30.00 | **23.9** |
| Missouri | 13.0 | 36.00 | **23.0** |
| Massachusetts | 42.3 | 29.00 | 13.3 |
| Washington | 64.5 | 51.25 | 13.3 |

Three files also contradicted themselves. **South Carolina's** Dimension Scores
tab said 32.75 while its own State Comparison tab said 39.75. **Virginia** held
three values for one dimension: 60 in its calculations, 80 in its comparison
tab, 50 in the master CSV.

### "VERIFIED" labels that meant nothing

**Nebraska** flagged both of its D5 sub-components as ESTIMATED while flagging
the D5 total that derives from them as **VERIFIED**. The audit's rule — "a file
labelling a value Verified proves nothing; judge only by what the cited source
says" — existed because of cases like this.

### Two states where the old data was demonstrably wrong

**North Carolina** was scored 30.0, and its workbook recorded that statewide
recognition events "could not be identified" and were "assumed zero." In the
rebuild, North Carolina scores **100.0** — its Office of State Human Resources
publishes a formal Rewards and Recognition policy under HR governance, an
Excellence in Service awards program, and a 2025 Governor's Awards for Excellence
honoree page. The infrastructure was always there; the old collection did not
find it and recorded the failure as an absence.

**Montana** was scored 19.0 — the lowest in the country. In the rebuild it scores
**85.0**. Its State Human Resources Division has run the Governor's Awards for
Excellence in Performance since 1990; the 2025 ceremony recognized **259
employees** plus 4 Outstanding Service recipients.

### Verdict

The audit returned **REBUILD** — its most severe verdict — and rejected REPAIR
explicitly. Its reasoning: repair assumes the collected values are mostly right
and the errors are isolated. Here the *definition* of 40% of the dimension was
wrong, four-fifths of the entries on that component were placeholder zeros, and
the sourcing layer could not be checked at all without redoing the research.
"There is not enough salvageable signal to patch."

## 4. Design principles of the new framework

Each principle exists to prevent a specific failure found in the audit.

### Binary indicators instead of continuous judgment scores

The old instrument asked for a 0–100 rubric score per sub-component. That
created constant pressure to produce a number when the evidence was thin — which
is how 43% of values ended up INFERRED. Four different methods were used for
"formal programs" alone, including a boolean shortcut that scored Delaware and
Pennsylvania 100 while 21 other states worked through a graded rubric.

The rebuild asks six **yes-or-no questions** with fixed point values. There is no
partial credit, so there is nothing to estimate. *Prevents: inferred values,
inconsistent normalization.*

### Every point carries a URL that was fetched and read

A YES requires the source document to be opened, and the supporting sentence
recorded in an `evidence_quote` field. Search-result snippets never score.
*Prevents: the 0-of-51 unverifiable sourcing.*

### Three answers, not two

| Answer | Meaning | Effect on score |
|---|---|---|
| **YES** | Qualifying document found and read | Points awarded |
| **NO** | Protocol completed; it does not exist, or the only evidence predates the recency floor | 0 points — **a real measurement** |
| **COULDNT_CHECK** | Protocol could not be completed — site refused, archive not public, file unreadable | Points **removed from the denominator** |

This is the mechanism that makes inference structurally impossible. In a
two-answer system, "we couldn't find out" has nowhere to go but into the same
cell as "there is nothing there" — which is exactly how 41 placeholder zeros
became published scores. With a third answer, an unknown stays visible as an
unknown, in its own row, with the blocker named.

### Identical protocol and an equal time cap

Four search steps, in the same order, for every jurisdiction, capped at roughly
10 minutes. The specification is explicit about why: *"Never extend the search
for one state and not others. Equal effort is what makes a NO in Delaware mean
the same thing as a NO in California."* *Prevents: an artifact of effort being
read as a finding about a state.*

### A three-year recency floor

For I4, I5 and I6 — the activity indicators — evidence must be dated **2023 or
later**. Older evidence scores NO, with the old date recorded. I1 and I2 have no
floor, because statutes and standing policies persist.

This rule has teeth. It is why Maine (proclamation 2022), Utah (2021), Minnesota
(1988 and 1990) and Oklahoma's 2008 document score NO on I5 despite having real
proclamations on file.

### Scores are generated, never maintained

`d5_scores.csv` is produced by `d5_score.py` reading `d5_evidence.csv`. The
script re-runs the structural validation first and **refuses to score a
malformed file.** Re-running it reproduces the output byte-for-byte. To correct
a score you fix the evidence row and regenerate. *Prevents: the 24% master-vs-
workbook conflict class, permanently — there is only one source of truth.*

## 5. The six indicators

| ID | Question | Pts | What satisfies it |
|---|---|---:|---|
| I1 | Does a state law or administrative rule authorize employee recognition awards? | 20 | Statute or administrative rule text |
| I2 | Does a state body post a written recognition policy? | 15 | A posted policy document or program page from any state body |
| I3 | Is there a named statewide employee award program with a public web page? | 20 | A program with a name and a live page |
| I4 | Published honoree list **or** documented ceremony/award cycle in the last 3 years? | 20 | Honoree names, a recap, or a count |
| I5 | Was a PSRW-equivalent proclamation issued in the last 3 years? | 15 | A dated proclamation document |
| I6 | Was there a state-sponsored appreciation event beyond the proclamation itself? | 10 | Documentation an event was held |

**I1 — authority.** Does the law permit recognition awards at all? Accepts both
statute and administrative rule, because states place this authority in different
instruments. Kansas has an entire statutory section (K.S.A. 75-37,105); Utah's
sits in an administrative rule (R477-6-7). Both count.

**I2 — posted policy.** Whether the state publishes a written recognition policy.
**The issuer rule was loosened during the build** (see §10): originally this
required the *central HR agency* specifically, which failed states whose
recognition function sits elsewhere — Illinois' suggestion board is run by the
Governor's office, Washington's by the Secretary of State. Any state body now
qualifies. A bare statute does **not** satisfy I2 — that is what I1 measures, and
allowing it would make the two indicators duplicates.

**I3 — named program.** A program with an identity and a public page, as distinct
from an authority that permits one. This is where several states separate: Iowa,
New Mexico, Rhode Island and Wyoming all have I1 authority and no named program.

**I4 — recent activity.** **Deliberately loose**: either a published honoree list
*or* documentation that a cycle occurred. **Names are not required** — requiring
rosters would measure transparency rather than recognition. A press release, a
dated cycle with results, or a count such as "259 employees were recognized in
2025" all qualify. A refinement was added during collection: a *scheduled*
meeting, an *open* nomination window, or a cumulative all-time total is not a
documented cycle. A completed determination, a named recipient, or a dated count
is.

**I5 — proclamation.** A dated proclamation. Three near-misses were rejected: a
standing statutory designation is not an annual proclamation (Delaware's statute
permanently designates the week — scored under I1, not reused here); a
legislative resolution is not a proclamation; and a page *titled* "Governor
Proclaims Public Service Recognition Week" with no readable date cannot clear the
recency floor.

**I6 — event.** Documentation that something was actually held. An announcement
of a future event does not qualify. This indicator overlaps I4 substantially —
see §9.

## 6. Scope rules and what was rejected

Four tests applied to every record.

**Wrong population.** The award must recognize **career state employees**.
Rejected: volunteer awards (Michigan's Governor's Service Awards honors
volunteers, businesses and nonprofits), awards for elected officials, private
citizens, students, and county or municipal employees. New Mexico's Governor's
Awards for Excellence are for **the arts**. Minnesota's Governor's Safety Award
honors **employers**.

**Wrong issuer.** A university, county, city or private association page *about*
a state program is not the source. Two consequential rejections: Florida's
productivity awards are founded and sponsored by **Florida TaxWatch, a private
nonprofit**; Louisiana's Dunbar Award is conferred by the **Louisiana Civil
Service League**, a private nonprofit, and is open to municipal employees too —
failing this test and the population test at once.

**News evidence.** A news article counts only if it explicitly states the
document or event exists. General coverage that a state "celebrated the week"
does not. Multiple outlets running the same press release count once.

**Federal vs. state.** Federal award rules, OPM material and White House PSRW
proclamations are rejected.

### A worked near-miss: New Hampshire

Searching New Hampshire's rules for I2 surfaced a document titled **"Sw 700 —
EMPLOYEE RECOGNITION PROGRAM."** On its face this is an exact hit: a state
administrative rule, correct title, no recency issue.

It was opened and read. **The rule is issued by the New Hampshire Lottery
Commission and governs recognition for lottery sales representatives** — not
state employees generally. It fails the population test and was rejected.

New Hampshire's I2 is recorded as COULDNT_CHECK, because the actual candidate —
the Division of Personnel's State Employee Suggestion and Extraordinary Service
Award page — returned HTTP 403.

This is what the fact-check step is for. A title-match would have scored 15
points on a lottery-vendor policy.

## 7. Collection protocol

Identical for all 51 jurisdictions:

1. `site:[state].gov employee recognition award` → informs I1, I2, I3, I4
2. `site:[state].gov "public service recognition week"` → informs I5, I6
3. The governor's proclamation archive → I5
4. The central HR agency site — whatever the state calls it (DOA, DHRM,
   Department of Personnel, OFM) → I2, I3, I4

**Cap: roughly 10 minutes per jurisdiction.** When time is up, anything
unresolved is COULDNT_CHECK.

**Fact-check, required for every YES:** fetch the URL and read it; confirm the
document says what the indicator requires rather than something adjacent; record
the supporting sentence; confirm the date clears the floor. If the fetch fails or
the content does not support the claim, downgrade to NO or COULDNT_CHECK and say
why.

The equal-effort rule is the load-bearing part. A NO is only meaningful if every
state got the same search. If one state's absence reflects a thorough hunt and
another's reflects a quick look, the two are not comparable — and the reader has
no way to tell them apart.

## 8. How the score is calculated

**In plain language:** add up the points a state earned. Then add up the points
it was *eligible* for — counting only the indicators where we actually got an
answer. Divide the first by the second.

```
points_earned           = sum of points_awarded
points_possible_checked = sum of points_possible where answer is YES or NO
d5_score                = points_earned / points_possible_checked × 100
coverage_pct            = points_possible_checked
```

COULDNT_CHECK rows are excluded from the denominator. Because every jurisdiction
totals exactly 100 `points_possible`, the checked-points figure doubles as a
coverage percentage.

### Worked example: Tennessee

Tennessee's six rows in `d5_evidence.csv`:

| Indicator | Points | Answer | Awarded |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| I1 statute/rule | 20 | YES | 20 |
| I2 posted policy | 15 | YES | 15 |
| I3 named program | 20 | YES | 20 |
| I4 recent cycle (2025) | 20 | YES | 20 |
| I5 proclamation | 15 | **COULDNT_CHECK** | 0 |
| I6 event | 10 | NO | 0 |

**Points earned:** 20 + 15 + 20 + 20 + 0 + 0 = **75**

**Denominator:** I5's 15 points are removed, because we could not determine
whether Tennessee issued a proclamation. 100 − 15 = **85**

**Score:** 75 ÷ 85 × 100 = **88.2**
**Coverage:** 85.0 · **Status:** PARTIAL · **Unchecked:** I5

Note what the rule protects Tennessee from. Had I5's blocked answer been scored
as a zero, Tennessee would read 75 ÷ 100 = 75.0 — penalised 13 points for a
website we could not reach. The I6 zero, by contrast, *is* counted, because that
one was determined.

### The coverage floor, and its current setting

The specification includes a guard: **if checked coverage falls below 50 points,
publish `INSUFFICIENT_COVERAGE` instead of a number** — on the principle that a
ratio computed on less than half the evidence should not be presented as a score.

**That guard is currently switched off.** `COVERAGE_FLOOR` in `d5_score.py` is
set to `0.0` by editorial decision, so all jurisdictions with any answered
indicator receive a published score. Seven jurisdictions fall below the 50-point
threshold and are published anyway:

| Jurisdiction | Score | Coverage | Indicators answered |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Arizona | 100.0 | 20.0 | 1 of 6 |
| South Dakota | 100.0 | 20.0 | 1 of 6 |
| North Dakota | 66.7 | 45.0 | 3 of 6 |
| Massachusetts | 57.1 | 35.0 | 2 of 6 |
| Maryland | 33.3 | 45.0 | 3 of 6 |
| Washington DC | 22.2 | 45.0 | 3 of 6 |
| Michigan | 0.0 | 30.0 | 2 of 6 |

Restoring the guard is a one-line change (`COVERAGE_FLOOR = 50.0`).

**One jurisdiction cannot be scored at any setting.** Vermont's six indicators
are all COULDNT_CHECK, so its denominator is zero and no ratio exists. It is
reported as `INSUFFICIENT_COVERAGE` — arithmetic, not editorial judgment.

### The tradeoff, stated plainly

**A partially-covered state and a fully-covered state can both reach 100.0.**
Eleven jurisdictions score exactly 100.0. Three of them — Delaware, Hawaii,
North Carolina — answered all six questions and passed all six. Arizona answered
**one** question and passed it. Both print as `100.0`. The arithmetic is correct
in each case; the two facts are not equivalent.

This is why `coverage_pct` exists as a column, and why **a D5 score should never
be published without it adjacent.** The denominator rule is still the right
choice — scoring an HTTP 403 as a zero would be worse, and would repeat the old
version's core error — but it means rank position and evidentiary strength are
two different quantities.

## 9. Results and limitations

### Coverage achieved

| | Count |
|---|---:|
| Evidence rows | 306 |
| Jurisdictions | 51 |
| Rows per jurisdiction | 6 |
| Fully resolved (COMPLETE) | 24 |
| One or more indicators unchecked (PARTIAL) | 26 |
| Unscoreable (INSUFFICIENT_COVERAGE) | 1 |

27 jurisdictions have at least one COULDNT_CHECK row; **62 of 306 rows (20%)**
are COULDNT_CHECK.

### Sourcing

| Source type | All rows | Of the 155 rows that earned points |
|---|---:|---:|
| OFFICIAL | 163 | **143 — 92.3%** |
| NEWS | 12 | 12 — 7.7% |
| NONE | 131 | — |

`NONE` rows are NO and COULDNT_CHECK answers, which carry no scoring source by
construction. Compare with the old instrument: **0 of 51 jurisdictions had a
fetchable source for any D5 value.**

### Per-indicator results

| Indicator | Pts | YES | NO | CC | YES rate of those checked |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| I1 statute/rule | 20 | 39 | 3 | 9 | **92.9%** |
| I2 posted policy | 15 | 30 | 4 | 17 | 88.2% |
| I3 named program | 20 | 27 | 9 | 15 | 75.0% |
| I4 recent cycle | 20 | 23 | 24 | 4 | 48.9% |
| I5 proclamation | 15 | 14 | 24 | 13 | 36.8% |
| I6 event | 10 | 22 | 25 | 4 | 46.8% |

**I1 barely discriminates.** At 92.9% it is close to a constant — 39 of the 42
jurisdictions where the question could be answered have statutory authority. It
awards 20 points that nearly everyone collects.

**The descending pattern is the substantive finding.** Reading down that
column — 92.9 → 88.2 → 75.0 → 48.9 → 36.8 → 46.8 — **authority is nearly
universal and recent documented activity is not.** Roughly half of states cannot
show they recognized anyone in the last three years; only about a third issued a
proclamation. Having the legal power to recognize employees predicts very little
about whether a state uses it.

### Ties and clustering

**25 distinct scores across 50 scored jurisdictions; 34 share a score with at
least one other.** The largest groups are **11 jurisdictions at 100.0**, 7 at
85.0, and 4 at 70.0.

Two causes. First, six binary indicators can only produce a limited set of
ratios. Second, the denominator adjustment collapses distinctions: a state that
went 5-for-5 on 85 points and one that went 6-for-6 on 100 both land at 100.0.

### Incomplete coverage is a retrieval failure, not a finding

The 62 COULDNT_CHECK rows break down by blocker as recorded in the `note` field:

| Blocker | Rows |
|---|---:|
| HTTP 403 (server refused the request) | 39 |
| Unreadable PDF (returned undecodable binary) | 14 |
| Page rendered by JavaScript, returned navigation only | 10 |
| HTTP 404 | 8 |
| HTTP 5xx server error | 5 |
| Domain did not resolve (DNS failure) | 1 |

**None of these are findings about those states.** Several are demonstrably the
opposite. Vermont's notes name a Public Service Recognition Awards program and
Personnel Policy 7.1, both located in search results, neither openable — every
Vermont domain returned 403. Michigan's I5 note records **two proclamations
sitting in the search index** (2026-05-04 and 2024-05-01) that could not be
fetched; Michigan's published 0.0 rests on two answered indicators out of six and
almost certainly understates it.

### What this framework does not measure

- **Quality or generosity of recognition.** A $50 certificate program and a $10,000 cash award program both score the same YES. Nothing here distinguishes a token from a meaningful benefit.
- **Internal peer recognition that is not publicly documented.** If a state runs a strong peer-nomination system on an internal intranet, this instrument cannot see it. The original D5 definition included peer recognition systems; the v2 instrument has no indicator for it.
- **Storytelling density.** Also in the original definition, and deliberately removed — that was the HOPS component the audit found to be measuring the wrong subject.
- **Agency-level programs below the statewide level.** A single department's excellent awards night does not score. This was applied consistently: Louisiana State Police, Maryland State Police, the Maine Judicial Branch, a Georgia DCS ceremony and Oklahoma's OMES-internal program were all rejected as sub-statewide.
- **I4 and I6 are not independent.** Of the 19 jurisdictions scoring YES on both, **9 cite a byte-identical source URL** and 12 carry notes stating the I6 event is the same event cited for I4. Only two genuinely distinct events were found across all 51 — Missouri's Award of Distinction reception and Washington's cross-agency PSRW employee events. In practice these two indicators award 30 of 100 points for what is usually a single ceremony.

## 10. Changelog

Precise per-change timestamps are not recorded in any file. The evidence file
carries a single `accessed_date` of **2026-07-31** on all 306 rows; scoring
artifacts were produced **2026-08-03**. Changes are listed in the order they
occurred.

**1. I2 issuer rule loosened — 2026-07-31.**
*Original:* I2 required the policy to be posted by the **central HR agency**
specifically. *Changed to:* any **state body** qualifies — central HR agency,
Governor's office, a productivity or suggestion board, a personnel board, or the
same policy published in administrative rule. A bare statute still does not
qualify.
*Reason:* the original rule scored NO for states whose recognition function sits
outside the HR agency, which measures organizational structure rather than
whether a policy exists. Illinois' suggestion board is run by the Governor's
office; Washington's Productivity Board is chaired by the Secretary of State.
*Effect:* all ten I2 NO answers were re-examined. Four flipped to YES (Illinois,
Washington, New Jersey, New Mexico), two became COULDNT_CHECK once the loosened
rule brought an unreadable artifact into scope (Idaho, Wyoming), and four stayed
NO after a search for a non-HR state issuer found none (Connecticut, Iowa, Rhode
Island, West Virginia). Every rescored row carries a note beginning "RESCORED
under loosened I2 issuer rule."

**2. I4 refinement — during collection.**
I4's looseness (honoree list *or* documented cycle; names not required) was in
the original specification, not a change. What was added mid-collection was a
clarifying rule: **a scheduled meeting, an open nomination window, or a
cumulative all-time total is not a documented cycle.** A completed
determination, a named recipient, or a dated count is.
*Reason:* consistency. New York was scored NO for publishing only a cumulative
since-1946 figure, and Illinois for having rules and a form but no recipients.
Tennessee had been scored YES on notices of *future* board meetings, which is the
same category. The rule was made explicit and applied to all three.
*Effect:* Tennessee's I4 was re-searched and re-scored YES on different evidence
— the Governor's Excellence in Service Awards, which publishes dated recipient
pages for 2022–2025.

**3. `source_type` corrected — 2026-07-31.**
A value outside the specified vocabulary, `MIRROR`, had been used for statutes
read from third-party legal publishers (FindLaw, Cornell LII, public.law) when
the state's own site was unreachable. The specification permits only `OFFICIAL`,
`NEWS`, `NONE`.
*Resolution:* all `MIRROR` values were changed to `OFFICIAL`, on the basis that a
faithful reproduction of a statute is an official-source claim rather than a news
claim. The file now contains no out-of-vocabulary values.
*Separately:* COULDNT_CHECK rows that had recorded `source_type=OFFICIAL` for
documents they had **not** successfully opened were normalized to `NONE`, so that
`source_type` consistently means "type of source actually read." The blocked URL
and the reason remain in `source_url` and `note`.

**4. I4 retained after reviewing its yield — 2026-08-03.**
Once I6 was collected, the overlap between I4 and I6 became measurable: 19
jurisdictions YES on both, 9 with identical source URLs, only 2 genuinely
distinct events. The question was whether to drop or restrict one indicator.
*Decision:* both retained, with the correlation disclosed in §9 and in the
results report rather than resolved silently. Restricting I6 to events distinct
from the award ceremony would reduce it from 22 YES to 2, which is a substantive
methodology change rather than a correction, and should be an explicit decision
rather than a side effect of this build.

**5. Coverage floor set to 0 — 2026-08-03.**
`COVERAGE_FLOOR` was changed from `50.0` to `0.0` so that all jurisdictions with
at least one answered indicator receive a published score. This moved 7
jurisdictions from withheld to published, two of them (Arizona, South Dakota) to
100.0 on a single answered indicator. The tradeoff is documented in §8; the
