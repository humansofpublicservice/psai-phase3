What HOPS is

Humans of Public Service (HOPS) states its mission as humanizing public service "to inspire others and build the government we deserve." Its tagline, "Celebrating the Heroes of Public Service," describes its core activity: surfacing the stories of government employees, past, present, and future, who serve their communities. Founded in 2022, HOPS has interviewed 200+ teachers, firefighters, social workers, and government employees across 34 states.

The inspiration for PSAI

Those interviews surfaced the same complaints on repeat: "I love this work, but I can't afford to stay," "we can't fill critical positions," "the public doesn't understand what we do." PSAI exists to bring data to that celebration: to quantify what the stories were already saying, and give the case for investing in public servants quantitative backing, not just anecdote.

What PSAI is, and isn't

  • Is: an index scoring all 51 U.S. jurisdictions (50 states plus D.C.) on how well state government treats its public workforce, across five weighted dimensions built from verifiable public sources under an explicit evidentiary standard.
  • Isn't: a measure of individual employee experience, workplace culture, job security, or day-to-day conditions within any single agency (see the excluded scope under Setting the Foundation); a complete accounting of every form of public-sector investment; or a finished, uncontested product, rather than a working index open to a second opinion.

Why this level of rigor is warranted

HOPS asks states to be accountable to the people who work for them; an index used to make that case publicly must hold itself to at least the same standard. A flawed index used to praise or criticize a state government would undercut HOPS's broader credibility. The discipline documented throughout this dossier, never invent a value, never score silence as failure, trace every number to a source, is what makes PSAI defensible in public rather than merely published.

What we need from you

The Ask: Academic Review (a.k.a Phase 3)

Phase 1 set the foundation and tested how generative AI could perform the task. Phase 2 audited that work and applied a more targeted, data-driven approach to using AI. Phase 3 is this review: an outside academic read before either goes public. Specifically, reviewers are asked to:

  • Rule on the six flagged open questions in Questions for Reviewers, especially the composite's zero-fill inconsistency (Q01) and whether the dimension weights are defensible (Q02).
  • Assess whether the Phase 2 methodology is sound, dimension by dimension, given what the Phase 1 audit found (Methodology & Evidence).
  • Judge whether the AI-assisted collection is an appropriate method for a public civic index, and under what conditions (Q04).
  • Weigh a single composite score against five separate dimension scores as the better way to report what PSAI measures (Q06).
  • Flag anything in the foundation, the choice of five dimensions and how they're weighted, that a methodology rebuild alone cannot fix (Setting the Foundation).

The starting point

Setting the Foundation: What is the Index?

What each dimension measures, why it was chosen, and how it's weighted against the other four.

What the five dimensions measure

D1 · 30%
Salary competitiveness, cost-of-living adjusted pay, retirement benefits
D2 · 28%
Turnover/vacancy rates, average tenure, workforce data transparency
D3 · 22%
Trust in and sentiment toward the public workforce
D4 · 15%
Investment in public employees' professional development
D5 · 5%
How formally states recognize their public workforce

The aggregate formula

Composite = 0.30 × D1 + 0.28 × D2 + 0.22 × D3 + 0.15 × D4 + 0.05 × D5. Each dimension score is built from that dimension's own indicators, weighted and combined by its own rule. See the "formula" block in each Methodology & Evidence exhibit below for D1 through D5 individually, and How the Composite Score Is Built for how the five combine, including where Phase 1 and Phase 2 differ.

The foundation: why each dimension was chosen

  • Compensation. Treated as foundational: without competitive pay, a state loses talent to the private sector regardless of what else it does well.
  • Workforce Health. A direct measure of whether people stay: turnover and chronic vacancies are read as evidence of systemic problems, not isolated incidents.
  • Public Perception & Trust. Matters instrumentally: Phase 1's stated rationale ties public trust to both recruitment and employee morale.
  • Investment in People. A signal of whether a state treats its workforce as a long-term asset, not overhead.
  • Recognition Infrastructure. The lightest-weighted dimension by design: recognition programs reinforce the other four rather than substitute for them.

This foundation also excludes, explicitly: day-to-day workplace culture, individual mission alignment, career-advancement timing, rural versus urban conditions within a state, job security, and workload relative to staffing. None are measured in either phase, because no source publishes them comparably across all 51 jurisdictions.

Why these weights

Phase 1's stated logic: Compensation and Workforce Health carry the two highest weights as prerequisites, not peers, of the other three; without competitive pay and a stable workforce, the remaining factors become secondary. Public Perception is weighted next because it feeds recruitment and morale. Investment and Recognition are weighted lowest, treated as reinforcing signals rather than substitutes for the fundamentals. The weights themselves were pilot-tested rather than derived from data: Phase 1's version history records that compensation's outsized effect on scores became apparent during the pilot phase, with three states completed at the time the methodology locked (Maryland, Virginia, and Massachusetts, per Phase 1's own SOSI_Framework_v2.2.md), at which point the weights moved from equal-by-default to their current split and were locked. Phase 2 rebuilt how each dimension is measured but did not re-derive these cross-dimension weights, which is what reviewer question 02 below and D1's own internal weight-sensitivity test both flag as unresolved.

Foundation versus methodology

The foundation, meaning why these five dimensions, what each captures, and how they're weighted against each other, was set once in Phase 1 and carries through unchanged. The methodology, meaning how each dimension's data is collected, verified, and scored, is what Phase 2 rebuilt from the ground up. Every Phase 1 versus Phase 2 comparison in the next section, and in Phase 2: Corrective Action, is a methodology comparison: the how changed completely; the why and the what have not been revisited since Phase 1.

The methodology, tested

Phase 2: auditing the initial index

Phase 2 set out to audit the pre-built PSAI framework, its data, and its results, and to determine whether the scores collected for each dimension were accurate, methodologically sound, and consistent across every state. Four goals followed:

  • Ensure the accuracy of the PSAI data and scores
  • Assess the soundness of the PSAI methodology
  • Conduct data collection and review using AI-assisted tools (Phase 2)
  • Evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of using AI for data collection and analysis

The fourth goal is notable: evaluating whether AI-assisted collection is trustworthy at all, and under what constraints, was a stated objective from the outset, not a framing this review has imposed after the fact.

The first two goals, accuracy and methodological soundness, are what the following test actually measures. Each dimension's Phase 1 data was assessed against three questions:

1

Traceability

Could a number be followed back to a source that actually opened and contained that number?

2

Consistency

Did every state use the same indicators, definitions, and calculation method?

3

Directness

Was the value read from a source, or labeled estimated or inferred by Phase 1's own report?

KEEP: traceable, consistent, mostly direct
ENHANCE: sound but incomplete; fill gaps, standardize
REBUILD: sourcing unverifiable, or states not measured the same way

Claims vs. reality, all five dimensions

Phase 1's "100% traceable, zero errors" claims (Methodology page, Track Record section) were made project-wide, not per dimension. What auditing each dimension individually found follows. Four of five dimensions have a quantified figure; D4 does not, and that absence is stated rather than estimated.

How to read these numbers

A cell is one data point: a single indicator's value for a single state. The denominator below reflects how much of that dimension the audit actually checked, not all 51 states every time. D1 checked 150 cells: 30 of 51 states, covered mostly alphabetically (26 states A through Montana, plus four more out of sequence: New York, North Carolina, Virginia, Washington) until the audit ran out of context budget and stopped cleanly rather than thin out the remaining states, × 5 rows each (four sub-components plus a computed dimension total). The REBUILD verdict was still extended to all 51 because the failures found were structural, not particular to the states checked, but the 30-state scope itself was a resource stopping point, not a targeted sample. D2 was a 5-state pilot (15 cells, 3 metrics × 5 states): a planned checkpoint the audit's own instructions called for, with a full 51-state collection to follow, not an early stop triggered by conclusive results. D3 checked all 51 states across 2 metrics (102 cells). D5's figure is jurisdiction-level: whether any of a state's values had a working source at all, checked for all 51. A cell counted as MATCH only if the audit opened the cited source and confirmed it actually contained the value; everything else, no source, a source that didn't say what was claimed, or a value marked "estimated" or "inferred," counted as a failure.

Key audit findings

Measured against the audit's own four stated goals:

  • Accuracy. Failed across every dimension: as low as 0% of D5 values and 13.3% of D1 values could be confirmed against a real source.
  • Methodological soundness. Failed: no consistent sourcing hierarchy, inconsistent scoring rules between states, and a stated "never estimate" policy that was not reliably followed in practice.
  • AI-assisted collection and review. Completed: Phase 2 built one Claude Code agent per dimension, each bound by explicit sourcing and verification rules (see Phase 2: Corrective Action).
  • Strengths and weaknesses of AI. Both phases used AI for collection. The difference was not the tool but the guardrails around it: unconstrained collection produced fabricated data at scale, while the same underlying tool, constrained by explicit rules and fact-checking, produced a verifiable dataset.

The verdict

Every dimension failed at least one question badly enough to require a rebuild. What survived into Phase 2 was the idea of where data should come from (BLS, HUD, NASRA, official state sources), not any specific value.

The evidence behind this verdict, dimension by dimension, is in Claims vs. reality above, and in the per-dimension exhibits in Phase 2: Corrective Action below.

Phase 2 corrective action based on audit findings

Methodology & evidence

The audit findings indicated that the entire collection process, not just individual values, needed an overhaul. What follows is that overhaul in two parts: the rules every Phase 2 collection agent had to follow, and the process itself, stage by stage, compared side by side with Phase 1.

What was done to correct it: the five rules every Phase 2 collection agent followed

Both the audit and the rebuild relied on Claude, so guardrails were needed to keep AI-assisted collection trustworthy. A separate agent was built per dimension, each following these five rules, derived directly from the Phase 1 errors found.

  • Never invent a value. If not found, mark "Missing." Every number traces to a file that was downloaded and read.
  • Use only approved sources. Where a source was specified (e.g. BLS for D1), the agent used only that source. Missing data was marked missing, never substituted.
  • Use one method for all states. Same indicators, same calculation method, no per-state exceptions.
  • Store the evidence with the value. A URL or description of where each value came from, or a note that it could not be recorded.
  • Run stop conditions and checkpoints. Pilot runs of 5 to 10 states first, for manual verification before scaling to all 51.

The process, phase by phase

Six stages, mapped 1:1.

Process phasePhase 1Phase 2
1. Data collection ChatGPT searched the open web per state; sources varied state to state. A Claude Code agent per dimension pulls only from named approved sources (e.g. BLS/HUD/NASRA for D1); no open web search where a fixed source exists.
2. Validation Stated policy: benchmark checks, human approval checkpoint. In practice, inconsistently applied; estimated values passed through. Three-way answer enforced in the data structure: found / confirmed-absent / couldn't-check. No code path for "estimate it."
3. Indicator scoring Each component scored 0-100 on a documented formula; three of five dimensions (D1, D2, D5) used a fixed unequal split (e.g. D1's 40/35/25 across Salary/Cost-of-Living/Benefits), the other two (D3, D4) genuinely equal-weighted. Mostly percentile rank or fixed-threshold, unequal weights reflecting how much each indicator discriminates (e.g. D1's 5 indicators at 32/22/20/16/10).
4. Missing data Policy: score as zero, not estimated; also policy: never estimate or extrapolate. Both existed; audits found neither reliably followed. Excluded from the calculation and remaining weights rescaled, not zero-filled within a dimension (exception: the top-level composite, see Question 01).
5. Combining scores Dimension weights 30/28/22/15/5, fixed. Composite = weighted sum of five dimension averages. Same weights, same formula, unchanged. What changed is what feeds into each dimension score.

How the composite score is built

The formula, unchanged between phases

Composite = 0.30 × D1 + 0.28 × D2 + 0.22 × D3 + 0.15 × D4 + 0.05 × D5. Both phases use this formula and these weights; what changed is what feeds into each dimension score, not how the five combine.

Where the phases genuinely differ is how a missing dimension value is handled before it reaches that formula. Phase 1's stated policy was to zero-fill a missing component; the audit found that policy inconsistently followed, sometimes zero-filled, sometimes silently replaced with an unsourced estimate. Every Phase 2 dimension excludes a missing indicator and rescales the remaining weights instead. That fix stops at the dimension boundary: the top-level file combining the five dimension scores (PSAI_CompositeScores_Rebuilt.xlsx) still zero-fills for 9 states, the one place the old approach was not carried through the rebuild. See Question 01, the item this review considers most in need of reviewer judgment.

Phase 2 · independent audit

Claude Code collection agent, per-dimension rules

Every value traced to a specific cell in a downloaded federal file, each fingerprinted (SHA256) for reproducibility. Five indicators, mostly percentile-ranked.

The exact sources (all 4, named)
  • U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics: OEWS Research Estimates (pay level), QCEW 2024 & 2019 (market position, trajectory), CPI-U (inflation deflator)
  • U.S. Dept. of Housing & Urban Development: FY2025 Fair Market Rents (entry-pay-vs-rent)
  • National Association of State Retirement Administrators (NASRA): Contributions Brief + Plan Type Overview (retirement)
  • U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis: Regional Price Parities (cost-of-living deflator)
The formula: five indicators, individually weighted
  • I1 Pay level, 32%. Cost-of-living-adjusted average state-government wage (BLS OEWS), percentile-ranked. Weighted highest as the most direct read on whether pay itself is competitive.
  • I2 Market position, 22%. Government pay divided by private-sector pay in the same state (BLS QCEW), percentile-ranked. Chosen over comparing job titles directly, since duties and seniority differ too much across sectors for that comparison to mean anything.
  • I3 Entry-level pay vs. rent, 20%. Entry-role wages (BLS) against a one-bedroom Fair Market Rent (HUD), scored against a fixed 30%-of-income affordability bar rather than ranked. A yes-or-no affordability question doesn't depend on how other states compare.
  • I4 Retirement, 16%. Three checkable facts from NASRA: Social Security coverage, plan type, and employee contribution rate. Chosen over a subjective pension-quality score, which is exactly what produced six incompatible rubrics in Phase 1.
  • I5 Real trajectory, 10%. Five-year, inflation-adjusted wage growth (BLS, CPI), percentile-ranked. Lowest weight because a single five-year window can reflect one unusual budget cycle rather than a durable pattern.

What it does

Downloads federal source files directly and extracts each value with an exact cell locator. Every value is extracted twice, independently, with a six-part fabrication check confirming no row uses words like "estimated" or "derived."

Versus Phase 1

ChatGPT searched the open web per state, with human approval after the fact but no independent re-derivation from a source file, which is how commercial payroll-scraper numbers went unchallenged.

Open questions (4)
  • 9 states scored on 4 of 5 indicators (CA, VT, MA, UT, DC, WA, NY, HI, AR): retirement data isn't published as a single new-hire rate for them. Rescaled, not zero-filled, but these 9 are measurably less stable under weight perturbation than fully-measured states.
  • Health insurance and paid leave are excluded entirely: no federal source covers both, comparably, for all 51. Disclosed as a real gap: a state with excellent benefits and mediocre pay scores lower than its actual package deserves.
  • 21 states tie at the maximum on the entry-level affordability indicator, so that 20%-weighted measure does no discriminating work among them.
  • D1's own build documentation recommends publishing tiers ahead of exact ranks, given how much more stable tiers are under sensitivity testing.

Phase 2 · independent audit

Claude Code collection agent, per-dimension rules

Turnover, vacancy, and tenure collected only from official state sources; missing data marked Missing and folds into a transparency score instead of being estimated. Five states (Ohio, Rhode Island, South Carolina, South Dakota, West Virginia) genuinely publish nothing usable and score zero, a real finding now, not a guess.

The sources (by design, no fixed list)
  • Each state's own official workforce/HR agency report: different agency, different URL per state, deliberately (no single national database publishes this)
  • Reason Foundation: the one named third-party fallback, used only when no official report exists, always flagged low-confidence
The formula: two tiers, weighted by directness
  • Tier C, reported outcomes: 65%. Turnover (50% of this tier), vacancy rate (30%), and average tenure (20%), each percentile-ranked against other states that publish it. Weighted above Tier B because a reported outcome is the most direct treatment signal available.
  • Tier B, workforce transparency: 35%. Whether the state publishes workforce data at all. Carries real weight because for many states it's the only verifiable signal, and because measuring your own workforce is itself a stewardship signal.
  • A state missing one or more Tier C metrics has its available metrics rescaled to the full 65%, rather than being zero-filled for what it doesn't publish.

What it does

Applies a strict source hierarchy (official document, then recovered online, then a secondary source, then a named third-party proxy, then no value), with a median-vs-mean check and a dead-link check on every row.

Open questions (3)
  • Coverage is thin and uneven: turnover published by 38/51, tenure 27/51, vacancy by only 19/51. Most states are scored on turnover plus transparency alone.
  • A high rank doesn't mean high-confidence data. Pennsylvania ranks #1 despite resting entirely on a third-party proxy figure, a news-relayed vacancy number, and a higher-ed-bundled tenure figure.
  • Turnover isn't one measurement across states: some count voluntary resignations only, some include retirements. Percentile rank makes these scale-comparable but not definitionally comparable.

Phase 2 · independent audit

Claude Code collection agent, per-dimension rules

Rebuilt around perception of state government as an institution, a proxy for perception of the workforce, since almost no polling asks about public servants specifically. A poll score and a news-sentiment score, blended by how much data each state actually has. The recognition component is pending, not removed: a 12-state spot-check found 0 of 24 existing recognition values verifiable, so D3 = D3_perception for now while the recognition rebuild, and whether it belongs in D3 at all, remains an open question.

The 13 verified polls, named
  • UMBC Institute of Politics (MD), DataHaven (CT), SD News Watch/Chiesman Center (SD), Colorado Polling Institute, Winthrop University (SC), Colby College/Goldfarb Center (ME), UMass Amherst/WCVB (MA), Bowling Green State Univ. (OH), Texas Politics Project, LSU Reilly Center (LA), Rutgers-Eagleton (NJ), WYSAC/Univ. of Wyoming, Univ. of Rhode Island RISI
  • WebSearch + WebFetch: a fixed 10-query template per state, source for the sentiment stream in all 51 states (switched from the GDELT Project after persistent rate-limiting made it unusable)
The formula: two streams, blended by confidence
  • D3 = (cP × P + cS × S) / (cP + cS). P is the weighted-mean favorable result across verified polls on trust in state government as an institution. S is the share of favorable state-government news coverage, with neutral coverage counted as half.
  • cP and cS are confidence weights, not fixed shares: a thin or unverified stream (a small poll, a sparse news corpus) is blended in proportionally less than a well-supported one. Where no qualifying poll exists (38 of 51 states), cP = 0 and D3 reduces to the sentiment score alone.
  • Government-as-institution trust is used as the closest available proxy for perception of the public workforce specifically, since almost no polling asks that narrower question directly.

Recomputes every sentiment score from raw article counts rather than trusting subagent output, and re-opens a random 10% of each state's articles to confirm the recorded terms actually appear.

Versus Phase 1

Phase 1 mixed polls about individual officials into the workforce-perception score and never checked that a cited article said what was claimed.

Open questions (2)
  • The 13-vs-38 split must never be collapsed into one ranking: the dataset proves the two can diverge by 20 to 35 points (Ohio: 17% measured trust vs. a neutral-to-positive 50 on the news-tone scale).
  • News sentiment can skew negative by construction: coverage tends to highlight dissatisfaction, so states scored mostly on sentiment (no polling available) may read systematically lower than their actual standing.

Investment in People

15% of composite

Same pattern as every other dimension: estimated, inferred, unsourced values. States almost never publish training or professional-development budgets, so the old framework was scoring a figure that mostly didn't exist to begin with. Scored as (Training Budget + Tech Investment + Career Pathways) / 3, equal-weighted, the first component being the one states rarely publish.

Sourcing pattern
  • No consistently named source: training-budget figures inferred from loosely related web pages, confirmed as unsupported when both Claude and ChatGPT were asked to independently verify them during the audit
Full per-state citation lists: not yet migrated to GitHub (Phase 1 project, separate from this evidence repo)
Phase 2 · independent audit

Claude Code collection agent, per-dimension rules

Rebuilt around two things every state actually publishes: a count of confirmed statewide professional-development functions (training divisions, leadership academies), and the Center for Digital Government's Digital States Survey grade. Explicitly a proxy for commitment/infrastructure, not a dollar figure.

The exact sources (2, named)
  • National CPM Consortium: member roster, cross-checked against each state's own program page (e.g. aum.edu, doa.alaska.gov, asu.edu program pages)
  • Center for Digital Government: 2024 Digital States Survey results, accessed via GovTech (https://www.govtech.com/digital-states-survey-2024-results-announced, same report for all 50 states; D.C. is excluded from the survey, and although a substitute source was logged, D.C.'s Digital States points, D4 score, and composite contribution are all intentionally left blank per the methodology, not scored via a substitute)
The formula: PD-function count as the base, two small bonuses on top
  • dimension4_score = (pd_points + digital_states_points) / 2. pd_points is a base score by confirmed PD-function count (0 for none, 55 for one, 70 for two, 85 for three or more), plus a +12 bonus if the state has a CPM-accredited program and a +7 bonus if it has a non-CPM certificate program, floored at 20. Example: Alabama has 1 confirmed function (55) plus CPM accreditation (+12) plus a certificate program (+7) = 74 pd_points. Digital States points: the Center for Digital Government's A-F grade mapped onto a 100-to-0 ladder.
  • Chosen because no state publishes a comparable training-budget figure in dollars. CPM accreditation moved from the primary scored metric to a minor bonus on top of the function count, since accreditation status alone doesn't indicate the breadth of a state's PD offerings.
Evidence file with every state's source URL: dimension4_pd_revised.csv
D4 Agent · the collectorCLAUDE.md
What it does

CLAUDE.md documents a validation rule: after collection, count jurisdictions confirmed with a CPM program (expected around 40) and flag if the count falls far outside that range. This is prose guidance, not implemented logic in the collection agents; the final data shows only 21 states with a confirmed CPM program, roughly half the expected range, and no source file shows that gap being caught, flagged, or reconciled before the data shipped.

Versus Phase 1

Phase 1's D4 formula never used CPM data at all, it scored Training Budget, Tech Investment, and Career Pathways instead. What went unverified in Phase 1 was those three figures, not CPM claims.

Open questions (2)
  • 10 states show no confirmed PD function, but at least NH and VT's official pages returned access errors rather than genuinely having nothing, so this likely undercounts them.
  • The dimension can't distinguish a well-funded program from a nominal one: it counts presence, not investment level, by design (no comparable budget data exists).
DIMENSION 5

Recognition Infrastructure

5% of composite
40% of every score measured HOPS's own story output → 92.3% of earned points now rest on a state's own document
Phase 1 · original build

ChatGPT collected & sourced data · Claude assisted analysis · human reviewer set framework

None of the sources cited in the Phase 1 state reports could be opened. Many results had no citation at all. Scored as (0.40 × HOPS story density) + (0.35 × Formal Programs) + (0.25 × Observances), a fixed 40/35/25 split, not equal-weighted, and per the original framework, "Story documentation and sharing (HOPS)" was itself a formally listed component, confirming the audit's finding that HOPS's own output was being scored as the state's recognition infrastructure.

Sourcing pattern
  • Opaque internal reference tokens (e.g. 【599618054233774†L24-L27】) that resolve to nothing: zero of 51 states had a fetchable source
Full per-state citation lists: not yet migrated to GitHub (Phase 1 project, separate from this evidence repo)
Phase 2 · independent audit

Claude Code collection agent, per-dimension rules

Six binary yes/no/couldn't-check questions, each requiring a fetched-and-read source URL. A blocked lookup is removed from the denominator, never scored as failure. Coverage percentage published alongside every score.

The sources (306 total, no fixed list by design)
  • Each state's own statutes, HR-agency policy pages, named award-program pages, and governor's proclamation archives: one full set of six per state, found via an identical search protocol, not one shared national database
  • 92.3% of earned points rest on an OFFICIAL state document; the remaining 7.7% are NEWS reports used only where the state's own page was unreachable
The formula: six binary indicators, weighted by specificity
  • d5_score = points_earned / points_possible_checked × 100. I1 legal authority (20 pts), I2 posted policy (15), I3 named award program (20), I4 documented activity in the last 3 years (20), I5 a recent proclamation (15), I6 a recent event (10).
  • Points for any indicator that couldn't be checked (a blocked page, a dead link) are removed from the denominator rather than scored as a failure, so a state's score reflects what was confirmed, not what a server happened to return that day.
  • Weighted toward indicators requiring documented recent activity (I4 through I6, 45 of 100 points, each subject to a 2023-or-later recency floor) over bare legal authority (I1, 20 points, no recency floor), since the audit found authority nearly universal while actual recent use varies widely. I2 and I3 also carry no recency floor; an old, still-live award-program page satisfies I3.
Evidence file, all 306 rows with URL + quote: d5_evidence.csv
D5 Agent · the collectorpsai-d5-collect.md + psai-d5-audit.md
What it does

Requires a fetched page and a quoted supporting sentence for every YES. Example: a New Hampshire rule titled "Employee Recognition Program" was rejected after the agent opened it and found it governs lottery sales representatives, not state employees.

Versus Phase 1

Phase 1's citations were opaque internal tokens that nobody ever opened to confirm they said what was claimed.

Open questions (3), unusually well self-documented
  • The 11-way tie at a perfect score is explicitly called "the single most misleading feature of this dataset" by its own methodology doc: 3 states earned it on all 6 indicators; 2 earned it on just one, because the other five were blocked and removed from the denominator.
  • Two indicators aren't independent: of 19 states passing both "documented activity" and "held an event," 14 cite the exact same ceremony for both.
  • The coverage floor that would suppress thin scores is currently set to zero ("publish all 51," per the code comment). The methodology doc describes this setting neutrally as reversible in one line; it does not recommend changing it either way.

Phase 1 published 5 tiers; Phase 2 collapses to 3, following the "<60=Critical, 60-69=Developing, 70+=Strong" rule set during the audit. Both score 0-100, but the tier boundaries and count are genuinely different scoring artifacts.

What we'd like your judgment on

Questions for reviewers

Questions spanning more than one dimension, or sitting above all five. Questions 01 and 02 are where an outside, expert view is most valued.

01 · OPEN DESIGN QUESTION
What's the right way to handle missing data at the composite level?

Every dimension rebuild enforces the same principle: never score missing data as zero. But the top-level composite file that produces the public rankings contains the note "zeros used as a placeholder for unavailable or insufficient data," scoring at least 9 states as if a dimension had failed outright when the real issue is that no usable source exists for it. For one of them (South Carolina), reversing that placeholder and renormalizing the remaining weights moves its composite fully into the "Developing" tier, not merely close to it. What's the best way to handle this: a fix that doesn't score an unmeasured dimension as a zero the state never actually earned, but also doesn't quietly inflate a state's score by letting a weak, unmeasured area disappear from the average entirely?

02
Are the dimension weights (30/28/22/15/5) defensible, and how would we know?

They were chosen, not derived: Dimension 1's own methodology says so plainly, then stress-tests its internal weights with a 2,000-run sensitivity analysis. No equivalent test exists for the weights across the five dimensions themselves. Given Dimension 1 alone is 30% of the score, does the ranking hold if the dimension weights are nudged the way D1's own indicators were?

03
Does rescaling for missing data quietly reward not measuring?

When a state is missing a metric, its remaining weights are rescaled to what it has (rather than zero-filled). That's clearly better than the alternative, but it also means a state that doesn't publish a metric it would have scored badly on is never penalized for that metric at all. Is rescaling the right general policy, and does it need a stated limit (e.g. a minimum coverage threshold below which a dimension isn't scored at all, as Dimension 5's own coverage-floor mechanism could enforce, though it's currently set to zero and withholds nothing)?

04
Is AI-based collection the right method here at all, under what conditions?

Every dimension's fix is a stricter evidentiary process wrapped around the same underlying method: an AI agent doing the searching, reading, and scoring. The discipline is real and the rebuild is a large improvement. But whether generative AI is an appropriate primary-collection method for a public civic index at all, and what independent human verification rate would make that defensible, isn't addressed head-on anywhere in the source material. This seems squarely a question for this audience.

05
Five different local conventions for "this number rests on thinner evidence"

Dimension 1 uses asterisks and a stability range; Dimension 2 has a hard zero for five non-publishing states; Dimension 3 has a strict never-merge rule between two groups; Dimension 5 has a coverage percentage and a status column. Each is well-reasoned on its own, but a reader has to learn five separate systems. Would a single shared visual/statistical convention for evidentiary confidence, applied consistently across all five dimensions, serve readers better?

06 · THE AUDIT'S OWN RECOMMENDATION
Should PSAI report five dimensions separately instead of one blended composite?

This review recommends reporting the five dimensions separately rather than blending them into one composite score, a direction the Phase 2 audit's own documentation already pointed to: "Dimensions can also be reported separately rather than as one score... Averaging the two dimensions hides which dimension is primarily driving a state's rank." Compensation (D1) is measured on universally-published federal data; Public Perception (D3) rests on proxies that can be politically skewed. Blending them into one number obscures that difference. A state could rank low overall for reasons that are entirely about missing polling data, not actual treatment of its workforce. Worth reviewers' judgment on whether the single composite should become secondary to the five dimension scores, rather than the other way around.

Where this could go next

Future directions

Two categories: scoped research questions suited to a masters capstone, and organizational moves that would make PSAI a durable, ongoing index rather than a one-time snapshot. Several overlap with recommendations the Phase 2 audit's own documentation already made, flagged below, indicating internal buy-in rather than this review's opinion alone.

Capstone-shaped research questions

Survey methods · D3 · echoes the audit's own recommendation

An independent trust instrument for the 38 sentiment-only states

Design and field a single, comparable state-government trust survey where no verified poll currently exists. This echoes the Phase 2 audit's own recommendation: "Commission or locate institution-level trust polls for the 38 sentiment-only states." Would directly convert the weakest part of D3 into its strongest.

AI methodology · whole index

Does prompt design explain part of the Phase 1 → Phase 2 gap?

Both phases used generative AI for collection; the discipline changed far more than the underlying tool. A controlled comparison of prompt/agent-instruction strategies against a fixed evidence standard would help separate what the stricter process contributed from what a more careful prompt alone could have contributed, and would speak directly to how much of this result is procedural versus a property of the AI system used.

Data acquisition · D1

Closing the health-insurance and paid-leave gap

D1's largest disclosed omission. No federal source covers both, comparably, across 51 jurisdictions. A capstone could build that dataset from 51 state HR sources under the same sourcing discipline D1 already uses.

Applied statistics · cross-cutting

A formal missing-data policy for the index

Compare zero-fill, rescale-and-renormalize, and exclude-below-threshold approaches empirically across all five dimensions, and recommend one consistent rule, which directly answers reviewer question 03 above.

Measurement design · D5

Resolving the two-indicators-one-ceremony problem

Build an independent event-classification protocol to separate "documented activity" from "held an event," or design a recognition-quality rubric that goes beyond existence to generosity and reach.

Criterion validity · whole index

Does PSAI predict anything real?

Correlate composite and dimension scores against actual outcomes (state-employee turnover, retention, applicant volume per opening) to test whether the index measures what it claims to. The strongest possible empirical capstone: it either validates the whole project or tells us precisely where it doesn't.

Reproducibility · whole index

Composite-level sensitivity testing

Extend D1's 2,000-run Monte Carlo weight-perturbation approach from one dimension to the full five-dimension composite, which directly answers reviewer question 02.

Organizational moves for HOPS

Governance

A standing external advisory board

Formalize what this review already is: make outside academic input a recurring structure rather than a one-time package.

Transparency

Publish the evidence, not just the scores

The evidence CSVs, source registries, and methodology docs behind this dossier are far more rigorous than anything currently visible on the public dashboard. Publishing them alongside the scores would let anyone, not just this review, check the work.

Cadence

A defined re-audit schedule

Given how much changed between Phase 1 and Phase 2 within a year, an annual or biennial re-audit commitment would keep the index credible as data ages, rather than re-litigating trust from scratch each time.

Pipeline

A named university capstone partnership

The research questions above are real, scoped, and ready to hand to a specific public-policy or public-administration masters program as a standing project pipeline, not a one-off ask.
